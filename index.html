The central model

A conversational large language model (LLM) is built in stages. First it learns to continue text from a very large collection of documents. That produces a capable base model, which imitates patterns in its training data but is not yet a dependable assistant. Next, demonstrations and preference or reward signals shape it into a system that follows instructions and conducts conversations. At use time, the model generates one token at a time, conditioned on the preceding tokens in its context. This simple-seeming interface hides a complicated training pipeline—and explains both the model’s impressive range and its weaknesses.

From the internet to a base model

Pretraining begins with collecting and cleaning text. Common Crawl supplies a huge raw web snapshot, but raw HTML is not useful as-is. A production dataset is filtered to remove unwanted domains, extract readable text, deduplicate repeated pages, score quality, and balance sources. The goal is not simply “as much internet as possible”: low-quality repetition and junk can harm the training signal, while diversity and quality matter. Karpathy uses FineWeb as a public example of this curation work.

Text is converted into tokens, the model’s finite vocabulary units. Tokens can be words, word pieces, punctuation, or byte-like fragments; they are not identical to human words. A tokenizer maps text to token IDs, which in turn map to vectors the neural network can process. This representation is consequential: the model sees sequences of token IDs, not letters and meanings in the human sense. Token boundaries help explain why models can be awkward at spelling, counting characters, or manipulating exact strings.

The neural network is a Transformer with learned parameters. During pretraining, it repeatedly predicts the next token in document sequences. Initially predictions are poor; training compares predictions with the actual next token and adjusts parameters to reduce error. Over enormous data and many updates, the model’s parameters encode statistical structure that supports fluent continuation and broad factual, stylistic, and procedural behavior. The model does not store a searchable copy of the internet; it compresses learned regularities into its weights.

Generation and the assistant pipeline

At inference, a prompt is tokenized and fed through the network. The model assigns probabilities to possible next tokens; a decoding rule selects one, appends it to the context, and repeats. Temperature and related sampling settings affect how predictable or varied output is. Generation is therefore an iterative process, not retrieval of a finished answer from a hidden database. A base model continues whatever pattern is presented. A prompt that looks like a web page, code, or dialogue may elicit that kind of continuation.

To make an assistant, developers use supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on example conversations. The examples teach the model the roles and format of a dialogue and demonstrate helpful responses. Further preference optimization or reinforcement learning (RL) can reward outputs that people or verifiable checks prefer. The result is more likely to answer requests directly, but the training signal is imperfect: models learn what tends to be rewarded, not a formal guarantee of truth or sound judgment.

Capabilities, limits, and practical use

A major consequence of next-token training is hallucination. The model is optimized to produce plausible continuations, not to stop whenever it lacks evidence. It can confidently invent details, sources, or explanations. Tool use—such as searching, running code, or consulting a calculator—can ground specific tasks, but the system must recognize when a tool is needed and use it correctly. Context is also limited: the current conversation can act as working memory, while parameterized training supplies learned background knowledge. These are different kinds of memory and neither ensures complete recall.

The model has limited, indirect knowledge of its own identity and state. It does not automatically know which product, version, or runtime is serving a conversation unless that information is supplied. Its apparent “thinking” is generated text. Giving it room to work through intermediate steps can improve some tasks, and reinforcement learning can teach useful reasoning strategies, but verbose reasoning is not a universal guarantee of correctness.

LLM capability is jagged: strong performance in one task can sit beside surprising failure in a seemingly simpler one. Tokenization contributes to weaknesses in spelling and exact character-level tasks. More generally, fluent language is not the same as grounded understanding, reliable calculation, or consistent reasoning. The appropriate response is calibrated use: delegate tasks where errors are tolerable, inspect consequential claims, and use external verification for exact or high-stakes work.

Where training is heading

The video connects language-model reinforcement learning with systems such as AlphaGo. In closed, verifiable domains, candidate solutions can be checked against a clear outcome, allowing repeated trial and improvement. For open-ended writing and assistance, there is no simple answer key. Human feedback and preference models approximate quality, but are noisier and easier to optimize imperfectly. The speaker presents this as an important, evolving part of model development.

The closing outlook includes multimodal models that work across text, images, audio, and video; tools and agents that act over longer sequences; and continued changes in available models. The practical takeaway is to understand the full chain—curated data, tokenization, next-token pretraining, post-training, and inference—rather than treating the chat box as an oracle. The system is powerful because it has learned an immense range of patterns, and fallible because its objective and inputs do not automatically provide truth, exactness, or awareness.

1. What the video sets out to explain

The video takes a general-audience tour through how an assistant such as ChatGPT is made and used. Karpathy’s aim is to provide mental models: what is behind the prompt box, how generated words arise, what the model is good at, and where its sharp edges come from. His organizing idea is a sequence of stages. Pretraining turns internet text into a base language model; post-training turns that base model into a conversational assistant; inference runs the trained model to produce a response.

This framing matters because the visible product is only the last link in a chain. An assistant is not simply a database of facts, nor a human-like mind that happens to live in a text box. It is a learned system whose behavior reflects its data, representation, objective, later training, context, and decoding.

2. Building the training corpus

Pretraining starts with text gathered from the internet, with Common Crawl serving as a major raw source. The crawl is a starting point, not a finished training set. Web pages contain markup, navigation, boilerplate, spam, duplication, and low-quality or inappropriate material. Dataset construction therefore includes URL and domain filtering, extracting readable text from HTML, deduplication, quality filtering, and other processing. Karpathy points to FineWeb as a public example of this work and emphasizes that the result is far smaller and more curated than the raw web.

The target is a large, diverse collection of high-quality documents. Diversity supplies a range of topics and styles; quality filtering reduces noise; deduplication prevents repeated material from dominating. Dataset design is not neutral: it affects what the model sees, what it can learn, and which patterns become common in its output. Even a carefully assembled corpus is not a complete or perfectly balanced representation of human knowledge.

3. Turning text into model inputs

A neural network expects numbers, not ordinary written language. A tokenizer splits text into a sequence of vocabulary items and maps each item to an integer ID. A token can correspond to a whole frequent word, a word fragment, punctuation, or smaller pieces. The resulting sequence is the model’s operative view of text.

The token sequence is then mapped into vectors, numerical representations the network can process. This step gives the model a fixed input/output vocabulary. It also introduces an important limitation: token boundaries do not follow human intuitions consistently. A word may split differently depending on spelling or context, and individual letters may not correspond to individual tokens. That mismatch helps explain why an LLM can write excellent prose while being unreliable at counting letters, spelling under constraints, or performing exact symbolic manipulation.

4. Pretraining: next-token prediction

The base model is a Transformer neural network with many learned parameters. In pretraining, the model receives a sequence of tokens and predicts the next token at each position. Training compares those probability distributions with the actual text and adjusts the parameters to make future predictions better. Repeating this process across enormous text collections builds a model that can continue many kinds of patterns.

The objective is simple to state but broad in its consequences. To predict text well, a model benefits from learning grammar, facts, styles, genres, code conventions, and relationships between concepts. Yet the explicit target is still continuation. The model’s parameters capture statistical regularities; they are not a literal index of source documents. It can express learned information without being able to identify where it came from or whether it is still current.

5. Inference: producing text one token at a time

At inference, a prompt is converted to tokens and processed by the trained network. The network produces probabilities for the next token. A decoding method selects one token, adds it to the context, and repeats. The next choice is conditioned on everything in the available context, including the model’s own output so far. Temperature and sampling settings influence the balance between high-probability predictable choices and less predictable alternatives.

This explains why outputs can vary and why a base model behaves like a continuation engine. The prompt’s format strongly shapes what follows. If the context resembles a dialogue, the model can continue the dialogue; if it resembles a document, it can continue that document. The base model has not necessarily learned that every prompt is a request to help a user.

6. From base model to assistant

Post-training adds conversational behavior. In supervised fine-tuning, the model is trained on prompt-and-response examples. It learns conventions such as distinguishing user and assistant turns, following instructions, and producing useful answer formats. This shifts the model toward an assistant persona, but examples cannot cover every request and do not make the model infallible.

Further preference training and reinforcement learning encourage responses that score well according to human preferences, reward models, or verifiable checks. The model can learn strategies that lead to more useful outputs. But preferences are approximations: a polished, confident response may receive positive feedback even when its factual basis is weak. Post-training changes tendencies rather than adding a proof of correctness.

7. Hallucination, tools, and memory

Hallucinations follow naturally from the training objective. A model is trained to produce likely continuations, so it may fill gaps with plausible-sounding content rather than signal uncertainty. Fluency and confidence are not reliable evidence that a statement is true. Tools can help: a model might search for current sources, execute code, or use a calculator. Tool use improves grounding when the correct tool is chosen and the result is interpreted accurately; it does not remove the possibility of mistakes.

It is useful to distinguish parametric knowledge, learned into model weights during training, from working context, the tokens available in the current conversation. Context can contain user-provided facts and earlier turns, but it is bounded. A model may fail to retain or use material that is absent, truncated, or poorly cued. It does not have automatic access to a full database of its training sources.

Questions about model identity illustrate another limit. The model may produce a plausible answer about its name or version, but its generated response is not a reliable introspection channel. Runtime and product information needs to be supplied or otherwise made accessible.

8. Reasoning and jagged intelligence

The video discusses how models need tokens to “think”: intermediate text can give a model opportunities to work through a task before producing a final answer. Additional reasoning-oriented training can make such behavior more effective. But visible reasoning is still generated output, and more words do not guarantee soundness. The design of the prompt and the availability of suitable tools matter.

Karpathy describes LLM ability as jagged intelligence. A system may succeed at sophisticated tasks and fail at a simple-looking one. Tokenization contributes to character-level failures; other failures arise from the limits of learned patterns and reasoning. There is no single smooth scale on which fluency predicts every skill. Users should evaluate capability by task and verify exact or consequential results.

9. Reinforcement learning: verifiable and open-ended tasks

In domains with an objective answer, reinforcement learning can use direct checks. Math answers, code tests, or game outcomes may provide a reward signal. The model can generate candidate solutions, evaluate them, and learn from success or failure. The video uses reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 to illustrate growing interest in this approach and links it to AlphaGo, where game outcomes supply clear feedback.

Open-ended domains are harder. There is no exact answer key for whether a piece of writing is helpful, kind, or complete. Human ratings and learned reward models can approximate judgment, but they are noisy and can be exploited. Reinforcement learning in language tasks is therefore powerful but less straightforward than training against a verifiable result. The choice of reward shapes what behavior is reinforced.

10. What may come next and how to follow the field

The video anticipates more multimodal systems that process and generate text, images, audio, and video; better tool use and longer-running agents; and continued changes in model quality. It suggests following evaluations and public technical work to keep track of progress, while recognizing that leaderboards measure particular tasks and preferences rather than all-purpose reliability. It also distinguishes proprietary hosted models from open models that can be explored through public repositories and services.

The overall mental model is a pipeline: curate data, tokenize it, train a next-token predictor, shape it with conversations and feedback, then generate responses from a context. The pipeline explains both the model’s breadth and its limits. LLMs can be valuable collaborators, but they are not oracles. Treat their output as a product of learned patterns and imperfect objectives; use tools and verification when accuracy, recency, or exactness matters.

Chapter map and practical implications

The video’s chapters move in a deliberate sequence. It begins with internet data and tokenization, then explains neural-network inputs and internals, inference, and the GPT-2 training demonstration. A hands-on Llama 3.1 base-model example makes the continuation behavior concrete. The second half moves to conversation data and post-training, then to hallucination, tools, and forms of model memory; it explores model self-knowledge, token-based reasoning, spelling, and jagged performance. The closing run focuses on supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, DeepSeek-R1, AlphaGo, human feedback, future capabilities, and ways to follow or access models.

This ordering is useful because it connects observed behavior to how a model was made. For example, a base model that completes a prompt in an unexpected style is doing the task it was trained to do: continue sequences. An assistant that follows a request more often reflects additional examples and feedback. Neither behavior should be mistaken for a hidden, stable human personality.

Reading an answer critically

When an answer sounds convincing, separate three questions: Is the prose coherent? Is the answer responsive? Is it true? LLMs are optimized to produce likely text and post-training encourages desirable responses, so the first two can be strong even when evidence is missing. Ask for citations or use retrieval when the claim depends on external facts; check the cited source rather than accepting a citation-shaped string. For arithmetic, exact comparisons, or code, run a calculation or test. When the outcome matters, independent verification is part of using the tool well.

Why tools and context help

A tool can supply information or computation that the model’s learned parameters do not reliably provide. Search can address recency; a calculator can avoid arithmetic slips; code execution can test a program. But the model remains responsible for deciding what to call, forming the query, and interpreting the result. A wrong query or misunderstood tool output can still lead to a wrong answer.

Context is similarly useful but finite. It can hold requirements, examples, source passages, and earlier conversational decisions. Clear, relevant context reduces ambiguity and can provide facts the model otherwise lacks. It does not guarantee perfect retrieval from a long conversation. If a requirement is critical, state it plainly and verify that the final response follows it.

Interpreting reasoning behavior

Intermediate work can help because next-token prediction gives the model additional generated tokens to condition later steps on. The output sequence can lay out subproblems, carry intermediate values, and expose an error for correction. However, language that resembles a proof is still generated language. A solution should be assessed by its method and result, not by how reflective it sounds. For tasks with clear checks, use those checks. For tasks without a fixed answer, compare against criteria and human judgment.

Training signals shape behavior

SFT examples show a target style of interaction. Preference learning and RL then reinforce answers judged better under a scoring process. The reward signal is a stand-in for what developers want, not the goal itself. If raters prefer confidence or brevity, the model may learn those surface traits; if a benchmark rewards a narrow pattern, optimization can focus on that pattern. Verifiable domains reduce this gap because an external check can score correctness directly. Human-feedback approaches are necessary for many open-ended tasks but inherently less precise.

A durable takeaway

The model is best understood as a trained generator embedded in a larger system. Its abilities come from broad pattern learning and later shaping; its weaknesses arise from imperfect data, token representation, predictive objectives, finite context, and approximate rewards. This model supports neither blind trust nor dismissal. It suggests matching the task to the system, giving it useful context, using tools for what they do well, and checking results in proportion to the consequences of error.

11. A closer look at the training pipeline

The quality problem starts before model training

A web-scale corpus is not just a big folder of documents. Web pages are produced for people and browsers, not for neural-network training. They contain headers, menus, advertisements, cookie banners, comments, repeated footers, and code describing layout. Extraction must recover the central text while avoiding both junk and useful material that is structurally unusual. URL filtering can exclude known sources, but a block list cannot make every nuanced quality decision. Quality heuristics and deduplication help, but introduce their own biases.

The selection of training text matters for more than factual coverage. A model absorbs styles and conventions as well as facts. If low-quality copied passages recur many times, their pattern is amplified. If entire classes of source are absent or underrepresented, the model’s coverage is uneven. A high-quality corpus is therefore the result of engineering judgments about relevance, diversity, safety, and scale. Those choices shape the model before the first optimization step.

Prediction turns documents into a learning signal

For a text sequence, the training objective can be thought of as repeatedly asking: given the tokens so far, what token came next in the document? The network returns a distribution over possible next tokens. A loss function measures how much probability it assigned to the actual continuation; optimization adjusts the parameters so the actual token becomes more likely in similar contexts. This is repeated across many positions and documents.

The model does not need a hand-written grammar or an explicit list of facts to learn many regularities. Predicting text rewards capturing recurring structure: syntax, discourse, common knowledge, and patterns in code or other symbolic sequences. At the same time, this mechanism does not distinguish truth from fiction in the way a verified knowledge base would. If a false claim appears in text, it still participates in the prediction task. What the model learns is influenced by frequency and context, not a universal truth filter.

Compression and capability

The weights are a compact learned representation relative to the much larger training corpus. Calling this “compression” is a useful intuition, but it should not suggest a lossless archive. The model is not expected to reproduce every document or identify its provenance on demand. It learns regularities that support likely continuations. Some information is represented robustly; some is blurred, forgotten, or entangled with neighboring patterns. This is why memorized-seeming details can appear alongside confident fabrications.

12. The interface hides system choices

A chat product combines a trained model with system instructions, conversation formatting, decoding settings, possible retrieval, tools, and safety or product logic. The model receives a structured token sequence that may include role markers and prior turns; the user sees only a chat interface. A response therefore reflects the model and the surrounding application. The same underlying weights can behave differently when the prompt, tool access, or sampling setup changes.

This also clarifies why asking “what are you?” is unreliable. Unless the product supplies model identity or the model has an external lookup, the answer is just another generated continuation. The model can reproduce identity statements it has encountered or infer one from contextual clues, but it cannot inspect the deployment configuration by magic. Runtime facts should come from product documentation or system-provided metadata.

13. Understanding failure modes by task

Confident invention

A plausible answer can be generated even when the prompt asks for an obscure fact, a precise citation, or a detail absent from context. Since the model must continue with some token distribution, uncertainty does not automatically cause abstention. The application or training can encourage saying “I don’t know,” but confidence calibration is itself a learned behavior and can fail. For factual work, provide source material or ask the model to search, then verify against the original source.

Exact strings and token boundaries

People often treat a word as a natural unit. The network treats it as a sequence of tokenizer units. For many common words this mapping is convenient; for an unusual spelling, a made-up name, or a character-count puzzle it may be inconvenient. A model can reason semantically about the requested word while miscounting its letters because the computation operates over token representations. The safe workflow is to use code or a character-aware tool for exact string operations.

Uneven difficulty

The “jagged” profile means capability is not predictable from task complexity as humans perceive it. A long explanation may be easy because it follows familiar discourse patterns; a tiny exact transformation may be difficult because it requires a brittle detail. Benchmarks can reveal particular strengths, but no single score captures all user tasks. Evaluation should be local to the task and include representative edge cases.

14. Working with LLMs effectively

Give the model a clear job

State the desired output, constraints, audience, and relevant examples. If a task depends on a source, include the source or provide access to it. This helps the model condition on the right information rather than filling gaps with generic patterns. Ask for structured output when it will make review easier, but do not confuse format compliance with correctness.

Use the right external aid

Use retrieval for changing or source-sensitive facts, calculators for arithmetic, code for deterministic transformations, and tests for software behavior. These tools compensate for specific weaknesses. Their output should still be checked: retrieval can surface irrelevant material, and code can encode a mistaken assumption. A useful assistant workflow combines language flexibility with operations that have explicit results.

Match verification to impact

For brainstorming, a flawed suggestion may be cheap to discard. For a medical, legal, financial, or operational decision, a polished but incorrect answer can be costly. Review should scale accordingly: trace factual claims to sources, run calculations, test code, and have a qualified person review decisions where needed. The video’s general point is not that every generated sentence is suspect, but that confidence alone is not a sufficient quality check.

15. Reinforcement learning in more detail

In a verifiable setting, a model can generate multiple candidate responses and obtain a score from an objective check. A math answer can be compared with a known result; a program can be run against tests; a game move can be rewarded based on the eventual outcome. Repeated exploration gives a training signal for strategies that may be hard to demonstrate exhaustively. This is the key connection to game-playing systems such as AlphaGo: success is measurable through outcomes, and the model can improve through reward-driven experience.

For general conversation, quality is multidimensional. A response may be correct but too terse, safe but unhelpful, or eloquent but unsupported. Human preferences provide a signal about these tradeoffs. A reward model trained on comparisons can score new responses, and reinforcement learning can push the language model toward higher-scoring behavior. Yet this creates a proxy objective. If the reward model is imperfect, the model may learn to satisfy its scoring patterns rather than the deeper intent of helpfulness or truthfulness.

The distinction between SFT and RL is therefore pedagogically useful. SFT says, in effect, “here are examples of desirable answers.” RL says, “try outputs and use feedback to increase the probability of rewarded behavior.” Both can be combined. Neither guarantees that the learned behavior generalizes cleanly outside the examples, reward criteria, or training distribution.

16. Looking ahead without overclaiming

The video’s forward-looking section expects more multimodality: systems that accept and produce more than text. The same broad pipeline can be adapted to different data representations and combined so a model can reason across modalities. More capable tool use may let models operate external software or services. Longer interactions and agent-like behavior increase the importance of planning, monitoring, and error recovery because mistakes can compound over a sequence of actions.

These directions are presented as expectations, not promises that every model will become a reliable autonomous worker. Progress in a modality or benchmark does not settle whether a system is robust in ordinary use. As models change quickly, public evaluation resources, technical research, and direct testing help users maintain an up-to-date picture. Leaderboards are useful snapshots, especially when they describe their evaluation method, but a ranking cannot answer every question about reliability, privacy, cost, or fit for a particular task.

18. Final synthesis

An LLM assistant is not one mysterious act of intelligence. It is the product of several understandable mechanisms: a curated text corpus, a tokenizer, a neural network trained to predict continuations, additional training on demonstrations and preferences, and an inference loop that samples successive tokens in context. Tools and application logic may extend this loop. Each component contributes capabilities and constraints.

The same mechanism that gives the model broad fluency creates a temptation to overread its output. Because it can imitate explanations, it may seem to have verified every claim. But its training goal is not the same as truth, and its generated self-explanation is not direct access to hidden internal facts. Because its knowledge is broad, it can help across topics; because it is statistical, it can still miss an exact detail or invent one. Because feedback can improve useful behavior, an assistant can become more cooperative; because rewards are proxies, cooperation is not a guarantee of correctness.

A good user mental model is therefore practical: treat the model as a capable generator whose output can be shaped by context, training, and tools. For open-ended drafting and exploration, use its flexibility. For fresh facts, exact computation, and consequential claims, bring in sources, deterministic tools, and review. Judge it by demonstrated performance on the task at hand. This preserves the technology’s real usefulness while respecting the limits the video traces back to its construction.
